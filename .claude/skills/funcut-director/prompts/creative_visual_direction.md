# Creative visual direction — the film, not a template

This is a decision framework, not a storyboard template, preset recipe, or fixed visual ratio. Every video must develop its own visual language from its thesis, audience, subject, emotion, evidence, and available assets. Reuse a reliable production technique when it fits; never reuse a whole visual treatment merely because it worked in the previous video.

## The director's job

Think as a director, cinematographer, motion designer, editor, and sound designer together. First decide what the viewer should understand, feel, question, or remember at each moment. Then choose the visual form that communicates that job most clearly. Do not start by filling a quota of B-roll shots, motion cards, transitions, or presets.

For every meaningful beat, privately answer:
- What is the narrative job here: establish, prove, explain, contrast, surprise, orient, breathe, escalate, or resolve?
- What should the viewer look at, and why is that the strongest way to communicate this beat?
- Is the information physical/real-world, data-driven, spatial/relational, emotional, abstract, or typographic?
- What is the best medium for it, and what would be lost by choosing the alternatives?
- How does this shot change the rhythm, scale, colour, movement, and visual density of the shots around it?

## Choose the visual medium by meaning

Use these as flexible signals, not rigid rules:

- **Stock / real footage** when the story needs real people, places, objects, activities, atmosphere, documentary texture, or credible context. Search by the intended shot and action, not just the narration's nouns. Prefer specific, emotionally and compositionally useful footage over literal keyword matches.
- **Original motion design** when the viewer must understand relationships, numbers, changes over time, comparisons, sequences, systems, timelines, mechanisms, or an abstract idea that footage cannot explain cleanly. Build a bespoke composition around the actual information. Do not turn every sentence into a generic text card.
- **Charts and data graphics** when data is the evidence. Choose the visual encoding that makes the claim legible (comparison, trend, distribution, proportion, timeline); label sources and dates where supplied. Never invent figures, axes, or causal implications.
- **Custom typography / kinetic type** when the exact words are the visual event: a sharp claim, contrast, definition, memorable phrase, or key reveal. Highlight only the words that deserve emphasis.
- **Generated imagery/video** when a specific visual cannot reasonably be sourced and a generated image materially improves the film. Keep factual depictions honest; do not present synthetic scenes as documentary evidence. Use code-rendered graphics for information that must be exact and repeatable.
- **Hold / negative space / restrained frame** when the narration needs emotional room, a pause, credibility, or attention. A deliberate hold is a choice, not a failure to fill the screen.
- **Hybrid** when a real plate needs designed information layered over it, or a graphic needs photographic texture. Keep hierarchy clear; do not combine media just to show off capabilities.

A visual decision must be justified by the beat's communication job. If footage is merely decorative, replace it with a more meaningful visual or let the frame breathe. If a graphic adds no comprehension, remove it.

## Build a visual identity for this film

Derive a visual direction from the creative brief and niche, not from a preselected preset:
- Establish a coherent palette, type hierarchy, graphic grammar, camera language, texture, motion character, transition vocabulary, and sound motif.
- Choose one dominant visual idea and supporting devices. Keep enough variation for the film to evolve.
- Let the hook earn attention with a strong image, unusual juxtaposition, visual question, striking scale change, or concise designed reveal. Do not force a loud animated title onto every hook.
- Let the intro orient the viewer and establish the film's visual promise; it should not repeat the hook.
- Let body chapters change visual mode when the information changes. A human story may return to footage; a numerical proof may become a chart; a process may become a diagram; a reflective line may use a hold.
- Let rehooks create a new question or a meaningful change in scale, evidence, pace, or visual metaphor—not simply another title.
- Let the CTA be specific to the film's promise and use a clear, restrained action cue.
- Let the outro resolve the story and brand identity without an arbitrary generic end-card unless that is genuinely the best finish.

## Make original motion, not preset-shaped videos

Use the motion preset library as a set of building blocks and starting points, not as the director's creative ceiling. Inspect the preset inventory and composition capabilities. Adapt or author compositions with the actual content, typography, timing, hierarchy, palette, and movement required by this film. Use keyframes, easing, layered shapes, masks, parallax, image plates, and data-driven elements where they have a clear purpose.

Before committing a designed beat:
1. Inspect the composition and its text/assets/animation structure.
2. Render representative frames with the motion preview tool.
3. Judge legibility, hierarchy, contrast, pacing, and whether the design explains the idea.
4. Revise the composition before baking.
5. Bake the approved asset and place it at the intended timeline time; verify the imported result in the actual edit.

Do not count a generated motion reel as a completed edit. A rendered file that has not been imported and placed is not an executed visual decision.

## Use Maestro and clip-skills as active expertise

Load only the Maestro references and shot cards that match this film's medium and design problem. Use clip-skills references for the specific editing decision being made: story structure, B-roll matching, pacing, audio-driven cuts, captions, sound, colour, transitions, and review. Apply their principles to this film; do not copy their examples as layouts. The director remains responsible for synthesis and the final creative choice.

## Evidence-led stock selection and continuity

For every footage slot, describe the intended shot in concrete visual terms: subject, action, environment, framing, movement, light/texture, and emotional register. Rank candidates, inspect contact sheets, reject technically or semantically weak clips, and avoid repeated clip IDs and near-identical shots. Check shot scale, screen direction, camera movement, colour, and energy across neighbouring clips. If continuity flags a problem, change the footage or intentionally motivate the contrast; do not merely log the flag and continue.

## Rhythm and density are editorial decisions

There is no universal percentage of footage versus graphics and no fixed number of motion beats per minute. Decide density from the genre, narration, information load, emotion, and attention curve. A documentary passage may sustain longer footage; a data-heavy explanation may need several custom graphics; a sensitive reveal may need stillness. Enforce coverage and avoid accidental dead air, but do not fill every second with animation. A repeated treatment is acceptable only when it creates a deliberate motif or visual grammar.

## Required decision record

For each major visual beat, record a short visual rationale in the plan or an attached director decision log:
- **Job:** what the beat must communicate.
- **Medium:** footage, motion, chart, typography, generated, hold, or hybrid.
- **Reason:** why this medium is the strongest choice.
- **Style:** the film-specific art-direction choice.
- **Transition:** why the visual enters/leaves as it does.
- **Fallback:** what to do if the chosen asset or renderer is unavailable.

This is not a fixed shot list. It is evidence that choices are intentional. Allow the plan to invent new visual metaphors and composition structures whenever the story calls for them.

## Review like a director

Review the whole film in context, not isolated attractive frames. Ask:
- Does the opening create a question and pay it off?
- Does every visual add meaning, evidence, emotion, orientation, or rhythm?
- Are graphics accurate and readable at final output size?
- Are stock shots specific, varied, and continuous?
- Does visual language evolve with the story rather than repeat one template?
- Are motion and sound synchronized for a reason?
- Does the ending feel earned?
- What are the three weakest moments, and what concrete change would improve each?

Use the vision critic when configured, but combine its taste feedback with measurable checks. Revise weak moments and inspect the revised render. Never claim the film is complete merely because all tools returned success.
